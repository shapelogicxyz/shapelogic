Golden rhombus puzzles: the rhombic triacontahedron, its 30 pyramids, and a 30-stick ball

Every angle in these three objects, where it comes from, and what to set a saw to in order to make the pieces

30 October 2026 · The numbers were computed from the icosahedral symmetry group with a short script that builds each solid from its symmetry axes, assembles the 30 copies, and checks that they do not intersect. Sources are listed at the end.

Summary

  • One angle does most of the work. The golden rhombus has corners of 63.43° and 116.57°, and 63.43° is exactly atan 2: a rhombus of side s is 2s/√5 high, and the foot of that height cuts the side at s/√5. The same angle is the angle between two neighbouring 5-fold axes of an icosahedron, which is why it keeps reappearing.
  • The rhombic triacontahedron has 30 golden rhombi, every dihedral angle 144°, and two kinds of vertex: 12 where five acute corners meet (an icosahedron) and 20 where three obtuse corners meet (a dodecahedron).
  • The 30 pyramids are the solid cut from its centre to each face. Each has the rhombus as base, four identical triangles with angles 37.38°, 63.43° and 79.19° as sides, and a height of 1.376 s. Neighbouring pyramids meet at 72° to the base.
  • The 30-stick ball uses one stick: a bar with a 36°-36°-108° cross-section whose flat face is a strip of three golden rhombi (1 by 3, corner 63.43°), with the ends cut along the other rhombus direction at 72° to the flat face. The sticks run parallel to the six 5-fold axes, five to each, and enclose a hollow rhombic triacontahedron whose faces are the middle rhombi of the strips.
  • The truncated icosahedron shows up as a pattern, not as a solid: 12 pentagonal pits where five stick ends pinwheel, and 20 hexagonal regions where three sticks cross. Those are exactly the positions of its 12 pentagons and 20 hexagons.
  • The cubic cousins (John Kostick's Tetraxis pieces, shown at IPP32) are the same construction with 12 sticks of equilateral cross-section around a rhombic dodecahedron: 70.53° rhombi, sides at 60°, four stick directions instead of six.

1. The golden rhombus and atan 2

A golden rhombus is the rhombus whose diagonals are in the golden ratio φ = (1 + √5)/2 ≈ 1.618. With side s, the long diagonal is 1.701 s and the short one 1.051 s. Its acute corner α satisfies tan(α/2) = 1/φ, and the double-angle formula turns that into something much simpler:

tan α = 2·(1/φ) / (1 − 1/φ²) = 2φ / (φ² − 1) = 2φ / φ = 2

because φ² = φ + 1. So the acute corner is exactly atan 2 = 63.435°, the obtuse corner is 116.565°, and cos α = 1/√5. The right triangle with legs 1 and 2 sits inside the rhombus: drop a perpendicular from an obtuse corner onto a far side and it lands s/√5 along the side, at height 2s/√5. That height, 0.894 s, is the width of every stick in section 5, and the ratio 1 : 2 : √5 is the simplest way to lay the angle out with a ruler.

2. The rhombic triacontahedron

Thirty golden rhombi close up into the rhombic triacontahedron. It is the dual of the icosidodecahedron and the polyhedron that Kostick's Triacontahedron Box is shaped like. Its vertices are of two kinds, and keeping them apart explains most of what follows:

  • 12 vertices where five acute corners meet. These lie on the 5-fold axes, at distance φs from the centre. On their own they are the vertices of an icosahedron whose edge is the rhombus's long diagonal, 1.701 s.
  • 20 vertices where three obtuse corners meet. These lie on the 3-fold axes, at distance 1.473 s. On their own they are the vertices of a dodecahedron whose edge is the short diagonal, 1.051 s.
  • 30 faces, one on each 2-fold axis. Each rhombus is perpendicular to the axis through its centre, and its sides are parallel to the two 5-fold axes that are perpendicular to that 2-fold axis. That is the real reason the rhombus corner is atan 2: it is the angle between two neighbouring 5-fold axes (section 4).

Note that √(φ+2) = √(1+φ²) ≈ 1.902, which is the circumradius of an icosahedron of edge 2, so these expressions are the ones that come straight out of the icosahedron's coordinates.

Kostick's box. The Triacontahedron Box on kosticks.com is this shape built from six identical rectangular blocks, each faceted with four compound cuts of "31.7° at 18°". Both numbers are in the table in section 4: 31.72° is the angle between a 5-fold and a 2-fold axis (half of atan 2), and 18° is half of the 36° between neighbouring face normals. The same page notes that "30 sticks of wood can symmetrically surround a rhombic triacontahedron" as six blocks surround a cube, which is the ball in section 5.

3. The 30 pyramids

Join the centre of the solid to the four corners of a face and you get one pyramid; the solid is thirty of them with their apexes at a point. Because the two diagonals of the base end on different kinds of vertex, the pyramid has two long lateral edges (φs, to the 5-fold corners) and two short ones (1.473 s, to the 3-fold corners), but all four lateral faces are the same triangle with sides s, φs and 1.473 s.

To make one from flat stock, cut the rhombus and four copies of the lateral triangle; the base-to-side dihedral of 72° is the bevel on every base edge, and the sides meet each other at 72° along the long edges and 120° along the short ones. The star of five in the photograph is five pyramids around one long lateral edge, which is why it closes exactly: 5 × 72° = 360°. In the assembled solid the lateral faces are all shared, which is why the complete sphere locks: every pyramid is wedged between its four neighbours, one across each lateral face. A partial assembly like the stars in the photograph has nothing to hold it but the fit of its faces.

4. Where the angles come from

Every number above, and every number in the stick below, is one of a handful of angles between the symmetry axes of the icosahedron. The axes come in three kinds: six 5-fold axes through the vertices, ten 3-fold axes through the faces, and fifteen 2-fold axes through the edge midpoints. The angles between neighbouring axes form a spherical triangle with corners of 36°, 60° and 90°, and its three sides are the angles that matter.

Two things make these angles so tidy. The 2-fold axes include three mutually perpendicular ones, which is why 90° appears, and each pair of neighbouring 2-fold axes is 36° apart, which is why so many angles are multiples of 18°. The faces of the triacontahedron are perpendicular to the 2-fold axes, so its dihedral angle is 180° − 36° = 144°, and every plane that is perpendicular to a 2-fold axis is a candidate for a cut that will mate with a neighbour.

5. The 30-stick ball

The ball is one stick repeated thirty times. Its construction is easiest to see by starting from the hollow and working outward.

The stick is three rhombi

Take the rhombic triacontahedron of edge s as a hollow. On each face lay a strip that is exactly that rhombus extended by one more rhombus at each end, along one pair of sides: a parallelogram 3s long and s wide with the rhombus's own corners, 63.43° and 116.57°. That strip is the flat face of the stick. The direction of the strip is one of the two 5-fold axes that lie in the plane of the face, so every stick is parallel to a 5-fold axis, and the thirty sticks fall into six groups of five parallel sticks, one group per axis. Kostick calls these his six-axis sticks for that reason.

The sides are at 36° because the hollow bends by 144°

The overhanging rhombus of one stick lies in the plane of its own face of the hollow, and that plane continues over the neighbouring face at the dihedral angle of 144°, so it rises from the shared edge at 36°. If the neighbouring stick's side face is cut at 36° to its flat face, the overhang lies flat on it. That fixes the cross-section: a triangle with 36° at both ends of the flat face and 108° at the ridge, 0.894 s (the rhombus height, 2s/√5) wide and 0.325 s high. It is the "108°-36°-36° triangle" that kosticks.com gives for the six-axis stick. Each stick therefore rests on two neighbours (one under each overhang) and carries two others on its own sides, and the whole weave holds itself together with no other contact. The model confirms that no two sticks intersect with these dimensions.

The ends are cut at 72°

The ends are cut on the line of the other rhombus side, so the flat face stays a parallelogram of three whole rhombi, and the cutting plane leans in at 72° to the flat face. That plane is perpendicular to a 2-fold axis, which is what keeps the five end faces around each pit in the same family as every other face, and it shortens the ridge to 2.764 s (that is 5 − √5 times s). The end face is a triangle with angles 31.72°, 37.38° and 110.91°: the first two are axis angles from section 4, and the third is 90° + 20.91°.

For a ball of about 75 mm, as in the photograph, s is about 17 mm: the sticks are 50 mm long, 15 mm wide across the flat face and 5.4 mm thick at the ridge.

The weave is chiral. Each stick is rotated 31.72° from the long diagonal of its face, and the choice of turning it one way or the other on every face at once gives two mirror-image balls. The arrangement has the 60 rotations of the icosahedron but none of its reflections.

6. Cutting the stick

All thirty sticks are identical and each has a 2-fold symmetry about the line through its centre perpendicular to the flat face, which is the practical fact: turning a stick end for end on the saw table maps one end cut onto the other, so both ends are cut with the same settings.

  1. Rip the bar. Start with a bar whose face is 0.894 s wide and rip both long edges at 36° to the face so that they meet in a ridge. The section is the 36°-36°-108° triangle, 0.325 s high.
  2. Cut the ends. Lay the bar flat face down with a long edge against the fence. Set the mitre 26.57° off square (that is atan ½, the complement of atan 2, so the cut line runs at 63.43° to the long edge) and tilt the blade 18° from vertical, leaning so that the end face makes 72° with the flat face and the ridge comes out shorter than the flat face. Cut one end, turn the stick end for end keeping the flat face down, and cut the other end at length 3s along the long edge.

Checks that are easy to make with a square: the flat face is a parallelogram with both long edges 3s and both short edges s; the two end cuts are parallel; and one of the two side faces meets each end face at a right angle while the other meets it at 120°.

7. The truncated icosahedron

Cut the twelve corners off an icosahedron at the one-third points of its edges and you get the truncated icosahedron: 12 regular pentagons where the corners were, 20 regular hexagons where the triangles were, 90 edges and 60 vertices. Its pentagons are perpendicular to the 5-fold axes and its hexagons to the 3-fold axes, so its two dihedral angles are supplements of two angles from section 4, and the thirty edges between hexagons are the middle thirds of the icosahedron's edges, each centred on a 2-fold axis.

The stick ball is not a truncated icosahedron as a solid; its outer surface is thirty roofs. What it shares with the truncated icosahedron is the layout of its features. The twelve 5-fold axes each pass through a pentagonal pit where five stick ends pinwheel, and the twenty 3-fold axes each pass through a place where three sticks cross and the ends of three more arrive, which reads as a hexagon. That is the same census as the solid's faces, 12 pentagons on the 5-fold axes and 20 hexagons on the 3-fold axes, and drawing the solid's edges over the weave makes the correspondence clear. (The solid that really is a trimmed triacontahedron is the chamfered dodecahedron, with 12 pentagons and 30 hexagons; it looks similar but is a different polyhedron.)

8. The cubic cousins: Tetraxis and the IPP32 entries

John Kostick's Tetraxis pieces, and the three puzzles he showed at the 32nd International Puzzle Party in 2012, are the same construction carried out with the symmetry of the cube instead of the icosahedron. Everything in sections 4 to 6 has a cubic translation, and the table lines them up.

Kostick's explanation of why the Tetraxis stick is an equilateral prism, that "the shape of the space it passes through is an equilateral triangle", is the cubic version of the 36° argument in section 5. He also points out that triangular sticks in these four directions and rhombic dodecahedron blocks fill space together, and that either kind alone fills space too. The three IPP32 entries are finite pieces of that packing, held by embedded magnets:

  • Double Duals. 24 sticks and 15 blocks, all sticks one shape and all blocks one shape, differing only in where the magnets sit. They build two things: a "jack" whose blocks sit at the six corners of an octahedron, and a "box" whose blocks sit at the eight corners of a cube, the duality of the title. Either fits inside the other, and the puzzle is to hide the fifteenth (ebony) block in the centre of the jack, put the jack in the box, then invert the box, spread the jack's sticks outward and pack everything around the black block.
  • Chamfered Cube. 24 sticks (a short set and a long set) and nine blocks: eight standard blocks and a small holly cube with its edges bevelled at 45°. The short sticks attach three to a block; the long sticks are an assembly like Kostick's 1965 Tetraxis Star, in which blocks hold four sets of three parallel sticks. Together they make a large chamfered cube, and with both stick sets in place each of its six square faces carries an indented pyramid.
  • 3-Layer Tetraxis Array. Three nesting assemblies. Layer 1 is one block with twelve sticks around it (twelve magnets in the block, one in each stick), the Tetraxis itself with its rhombic dodecahedron hollow filled. Layer 2 is twelve sticks and twelve blocks, the next shell out, and Layer 3 continues it. Each layer can be shown alone or around the others, but only when Layer 1 is turned the right way, which is the same chirality that section 5 noted for the ball.

Kostick's wooden six-axis set, the thirty-stick version of the same idea, is also sold with a six-block rhombic triacontahedron that fills the centre of the weave; that box in turn has a cubic hollow of its own.
